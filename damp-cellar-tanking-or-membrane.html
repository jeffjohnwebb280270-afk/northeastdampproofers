Damp cellar: is it normal, and do you need tanking?

By Jeff Webb, PCA Qualified Technician in Damp & Timber · 11 November 2026

Short answer: some dampness in an old, unconverted cellar is normal. It's below ground, it's built from brick or stone with no waterproofing, and the soil around it is wet for much of the year. Whether you need to do anything depends on what you want to use the space for. If it's just for storing the bins and the Christmas decorations, better ventilation and keeping gutters and drains working may be enough. If you want a dry storage room, an office or a bedroom, the cellar needs a proper waterproofing system designed for it, and that means either tanking or a cavity drain membrane.

Why old cellars are damp

A lot of Victorian and Edwardian houses in Newcastle, Gateshead, Sunderland and Durham have cellars, from coal cellars under terraces to full basements under bigger Victorian villas. They were never designed to be dry living space.

Water gets into a cellar in a few ways:

  • Ground moisture soaking through the walls and floor, because the soil against them is damp
  • Water under pressure after prolonged rain, when the water table rises or water pools against the walls. This is when you see wet floors and trickles
  • Leaks from outside: blocked gullies, broken drains, downpipes that discharge against the wall, or a leaking rising main
  • Condensation: cellars are cold, so warm air coming down from the house condenses on the walls. It's especially common in summer, which surprises people

What's normal and what isn't

In an unconverted cellar, these are pretty usual:

  • Damp-looking walls with some white salt deposits
  • A musty smell
  • Flaking limewash or paint
  • Some dampness on the floor after a long wet spell

These are worth getting looked at sooner rather than later:

  • Standing water or a cellar that floods
  • Damp appearing suddenly in a cellar that has always been fairly dry, which often points to a leaking drain or water pipe
  • Rot in floor joists, timber lintels or the stairs, or fungus on timber. A damp cellar under a suspended timber floor can lead to wet rot or dry rot in the timbers above. Our guide to dry rot and wet rot explains how to tell them apart
  • Damp, mould or a musty smell spreading into the ground-floor rooms

Quick things to check yourself

  • Are the gutters and downpipes clear, and does the water go into a working drain rather than against the wall?
  • Is there a gully or drain near the cellar wall that's blocked or overflowing?
  • Have the old air bricks or the coal-hole vent been blocked up?
  • Is the water meter or stopcock showing usage when nothing is running?
  • Is the cellar door kept shut, so damp air isn't moving up into the house and warm house air isn't condensing down there?

None of this will make a cellar dry enough to live in, but it can make a real difference to a storage cellar, and there's no point waterproofing while a broken drain pours water against the wall.

Tanking or membrane: what's the difference?

Below-ground waterproofing in the UK is designed to the British Standard BS 8102, which describes three types of protection:

  • Type A (barrier protection), often called tanking. A waterproof render, slurry or coating is applied to the walls and floor to physically hold the water back.
  • Type B (structurally integral). The structure itself, usually watertight concrete, keeps water out. This is for new builds, not old brick or stone cellars.
  • Type C (drained protection), a cavity drain membrane system. A studded plastic membrane is fixed to the walls and floor. Water that comes through the wall is allowed in behind the membrane, collected in a drainage channel and taken away, usually to a sump and pump.

The standard allows these to be combined where needed. In existing cellars, the choice usually comes down to tanking or a membrane:

  • Tanking has to resist the full water pressure, so the wall surface has to be sound and well prepared, and any crack or weak spot can let water through. It can suit cellars with damp walls rather than running water.
  • Cavity drain membranes don't try to fight the water, they manage it. That makes them more forgiving on uneven old brick and stone, and they're commonly used where there's more water. They do need the drainage and any pump to be maintained, and the channels need to be accessible so they can be cleared. A sump pump that fails or loses power is the weak point, so ask about a battery backup and a service plan.

Neither is automatically better. What matters is that the system is designed for that cellar, its water levels and what it will be used for.

Converting a cellar into a room

If you're planning to turn a cellar into a living space, waterproofing is only one part of it. Building Regulations apply to cellar conversions, covering things like fire escape, ventilation, ceiling height, damp proofing and electrics, so speak to building control before you start. The Planning Portal says converting an existing residential cellar into living space is in most cases unlikely to need planning permission, as long as it isn't a separate flat, the use doesn't change significantly and you're not adding a light well. Listed buildings are different, and if your house shares walls with neighbours the Party Wall Act may apply. Check with your council if in doubt.

Cowboy warnings

  • Be wary of anyone offering to "inject a damp-proof course" into cellar walls. Chemical DPCs are for rising damp in walls above ground; they won't stop water coming through a wall that has soil behind it
  • A quote that doesn't say where the water will go, or what happens if a pump fails, isn't a design
  • Get a written specification and ask what guarantee is offered and who stands behind it

When to get a survey

Get a cellar looked at if it floods or has standing water, if you've noticed rot in the timbers above, if you're buying a house with a damp cellar and want to know what you're taking on (see our guide to damp on a homebuyer survey), or before you spend money converting it.

Common questions

Is it normal for a cellar to be damp?

Yes, in an old unconverted cellar some dampness is normal, because the walls are below ground and have no waterproofing. It only needs treating if water is pooling, timbers are rotting, damp is spreading into the house, or you want to use the space for something that needs it dry.

Is tanking or a cavity drain membrane better for a cellar?

Neither is better in every case. Tanking holds water back and needs a sound, well-prepared wall. A cavity drain membrane lets water in behind it and drains it away, which suits uneven old walls and wetter cellars, but its drainage and any pump must be maintained. The right choice depends on the cellar and how it will be used.

Can a damp-proof course be injected into cellar walls?

Injected damp-proof courses are designed to stop rising damp in walls above ground. They won't stop water passing through a cellar wall that has soil against it. Below-ground walls need a waterproofing system designed to BS 8102.
