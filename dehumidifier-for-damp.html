Will a dehumidifier fix damp? What it can and can't do

By Jeff Webb, PCA Qualified Technician in Damp & Timber · 16 November 2026

Short answer: a dehumidifier is very good at one job: pulling moisture out of the air. That makes it a real help with condensation and mould, and with drying a house out after a leak or new plaster. It will not fix rising damp or penetrating damp, because the water in those walls is coming from the ground or from outside, and it will keep coming no matter how dry you make the air in the room. Use one to manage the symptoms while you find and fix the cause, not instead of it.

Why everyone's asking this now

Once the heating goes on and the windows stay shut, we hear the same thing across Newcastle, Gateshead, Sunderland and Durham: "I've bought a dehumidifier, it fills up every day, but the wall's still damp." A full tank feels like proof it's working, and in one sense it is. But how much water it collects tells you how wet the air is. It doesn't tell you why a wall is wet.

Where a dehumidifier genuinely helps

  • Condensation. If you get water running down the windows in the morning and black mould in corners, lowering the humidity in the room means less moisture settles on cold surfaces. This is where a dehumidifier earns its keep.
  • Drying washing indoors. Plenty of North East flats and terraces have nowhere to dry clothes outside in winter. Running a dehumidifier in a closed room with the washing stops all that water ending up on your walls and windows.
  • Drying out after a leak. After a burst pipe, a leaking roof or an overflowing bath, a dehumidifier speeds up drying once the source has been fixed.
  • New plaster and building work. Fresh plaster and screed hold a lot of water. Gentle, steady drying helps, though forcing it too fast can cause cracking, so follow your plasterer's advice.
  • Cellars used for storage. In a cellar that's naturally a bit damp, it can keep the air drier for stored items. It won't stop water coming through the walls, which we cover in our guide to damp cellars and tanking.

Where it won't fix the problem

Rising damp. Moisture is being drawn up from the ground into the base of the wall. Drying the room air may make the surface look a bit better for a while, but the wall keeps feeding itself from below. More often than not, though, what looks like rising damp is something simpler, like a bridged damp-proof course or high ground outside. Our article on whether your damp-proof course has failed explains what to look for.

Penetrating damp. Water getting in through a blocked gutter, a cracked downpipe, failed pointing or a roof defect will keep coming in every time it rains. A dehumidifier is just mopping up behind it.

Leaks. A slow leak from a pipe, shower tray or waste will beat any dehumidifier. If a patch stays wet in one spot regardless of the weather, suspect plumbing.

The risk is that a dehumidifier masks these problems. The wall looks a bit better, so the real fault gets left, and meanwhile timber like skirting boards, joist ends and floorboards can stay wet long enough to start rotting.

Quick check: is it the air or the wall?

A few simple observations go a long way:

  • Get a hygrometer. It's a small, inexpensive gadget that shows the humidity in the room. Many manufacturers and housing guides suggest keeping indoor humidity somewhere around 40–60%. If your readings are regularly high, condensation is likely playing a part.
  • Look at the pattern. Mould spotting in corners, on window reveals and behind furniture, worse in winter, points to condensation. A tide mark low on a ground-floor wall with white salts points to something else.
  • Watch after rain. If a patch spreads or darkens after a wet, windy spell, think penetrating damp.
  • Run it for a couple of weeks. If the room air is drier but one patch of wall stays just as damp, the moisture is coming from somewhere the dehumidifier can't reach.

If you're not sure which you're looking at, our guide on black mould: condensation or rising damp? goes through the signs side by side.

Desiccant or compressor?

There are two main types, and which suits you depends mostly on how warm the room is.

  • Compressor dehumidifiers work by cooling air over a cold coil. They are usually the more economical choice in a warm, heated room, but they become less effective in cold spaces and can frost up.
  • Desiccant dehumidifiers absorb moisture with a drying material and tend to cope better in cool rooms, garages and unheated spaces. They generally use more electricity, and they give off a little warmth, which some people find handy in a cold bedroom.

In a lot of older North East terraces and Tyneside flats, the rooms with the worst condensation are the coldest ones, such as an unheated back bedroom. That's worth bearing in mind before you buy.

Getting the most from one

  • Close the door and windows of the room you're drying, otherwise you're trying to dry the street.
  • Keep it away from walls and curtains so air can flow through it.
  • Use the humidity setting rather than running it flat out all day.
  • Keep heating low and steady as well. Warmer surfaces mean less condensation in the first place.
  • Don't stop using extractor fans and trickle vents. A dehumidifier works alongside ventilation, not instead of it.
  • Clean the filter as the instructions say, and empty the tank or use continuous drainage if your model has it.

Renting?

If you're a tenant and your landlord's answer to damp is to hand you a dehumidifier, that may help with condensation, but it isn't a substitute for them looking into the cause, especially if there are signs of leaks or water coming in from outside. Our guide for private tenants with damp and mould explains where you stand.

When to get a survey

Get a proper look if:

  • a patch stays damp even though the room's humidity is under control
  • you can see tide marks, salts, or crumbling or bubbling plaster
  • it gets worse after rain
  • skirting boards or floorboards feel soft or smell musty
  • you're buying a house, or you're a landlord dealing with a complaint

Common questions

Will a dehumidifier dry out a damp wall?

Only if the wall got wet from condensation or a one-off leak that has since been fixed. If water is still getting in from the ground or from outside, the wall will keep re-wetting and a dehumidifier will only dry the surface and the room air.

Can a dehumidifier stop black mould?

It can help a lot, because mould needs damp surfaces and a dehumidifier lowers the moisture that condenses on cold walls and windows. Clean off existing mould properly, and keep up ventilation and steady heating too, or it tends to come back.

Does a full dehumidifier tank mean I have rising damp?

No. A full tank shows there's a lot of moisture in the air, which is common in winter from cooking, showers, drying washing and breathing. It doesn't tell you where damp in a wall is coming from. That needs a proper diagnosis.
