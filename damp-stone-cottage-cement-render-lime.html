Damp in an old stone cottage? Why cement and plastic paint often make it worse

By Jeff Webb, PCA Qualified Technician in Damp & Timber · 14 November 2026

Short answer: old solid stone walls were built to take on a bit of water and then dry out again, mostly through soft lime mortar and lime plaster. When that wall gets covered in cement render, hard cement pointing, gypsum or sand-and-cement plaster, or modern plastic paints, the moisture can't escape and it shows up inside as damp. In most stone cottages the fix is to find what's trapping or letting in the water and put it right, not to inject a chemical damp-proof course.

How an old stone wall is meant to work

A lot of older houses across Northumberland, County Durham and the Tyne valley are built with solid stone walls: no cavity, often two faces of stone with a rubble-and-lime core in between. Many were built before damp-proof courses were common, and some never had one.

Those walls were never watertight. Rain soaks into the outer face, a little moisture comes up from the ground, and the wall copes because the lime mortar and lime plaster let that moisture move through and evaporate from both faces. As long as the wall can dry out faster than it gets wet, the inside stays reasonably dry.

Problems start when something tips that balance: more water getting in, or less able to get out.

The usual culprits we find

  • Cement pointing. Hard cement mortar on soft sandstone stops the joints from drying, so water sits in the stone instead. Over time you often see the stone face crumbling around joints that look perfectly sound.
  • Cement render or pebbledash. Once it cracks, which it usually does, rain gets in behind it and can't get back out. The wall underneath can stay wet for a long time.
  • Gypsum or sand-and-cement replastering inside. Often done during an earlier "damp treatment". It can hide the problem for a while, then the damp and salts break through at the edges or the plaster blows.
  • Plastic and vinyl paints on lime plaster or stonework, inside or out, which form a skin moisture can't pass through.
  • Raised ground levels. Concrete paths, patios or flower beds built up against the wall above the old floor level, pushing water straight into the base of the wall.
  • Gutters, downpipes and drains. Overflowing or leaking rainwater goods soaking one section of wall. Often the simplest cause and the cheapest to fix.
  • Condensation on cold walls. Thick stone walls are cold in winter, so modern living (showers, drying washing, sealed windows) leads to mould in corners and behind furniture. See our guide on black mould: condensation or rising damp?

Why injection damp-proofing is often the wrong answer here

Injecting a chemical damp-proof course relies on forming a continuous barrier along a bed joint. In a random rubble wall with an irregular core, that's very hard to achieve, and building conservation bodies such as the SPAB advise caution about relying on it in traditional buildings. It's also usually followed by hacking off the old plaster and replastering in a cement-based or waterproof system, which can push the moisture somewhere else in the wall.

That doesn't mean a stone cottage can never have a genuine ground-moisture problem. But in my experience the walls are far more often wet because of what's been done to them, or around them, than because they "need" a damp-proof course. If you're being told it has failed, it's worth reading has my damp-proof course failed? first.

Things you can check yourself

  • Walk round the outside after heavy rain. Are gutters overflowing, downpipes leaking or discharging against the wall?
  • Is the ground, path or patio outside higher than the floor inside? Is anything piled against the wall?
  • Look at the pointing. Grey, hard, smeared cement on soft stone is a warning sign, especially if the stone round it is eroding.
  • Is the house rendered? Look for cracks, hollow-sounding patches and staining below them.
  • Inside, does the damp line up with something outside (a downpipe, a raised bed, a cracked sill)?
  • Is the damp worse in winter and on the coldest walls, with black mould spots? That points more towards condensation.

What usually works on stone walls

  • Fixing rainwater goods and drainage first, and getting the ground level outside back down below the internal floor
  • Raking out cement pointing and repointing in a suitable lime mortar, done carefully so the stone isn't damaged
  • Removing failed cement render where it's trapping water, and deciding with the owner what goes back on
  • Replacing gypsum or cement plaster on damp sections with lime plaster and breathable finishes
  • Better ventilation and steadier heating where condensation is part of the picture
  • Being patient: a thick stone wall that's been wet for years can take a long time to dry out, so judge the result over seasons, not weeks

If you're thinking about insulating the inside of a stone wall, get advice first. Choosing the wrong system can trap moisture against the cold stone.

Listed buildings and conservation areas

If your cottage is listed, many of these works, including damp-proofing, replastering, removing render and repointing, are likely to need listed building consent. Carrying out unauthorised works to a listed building can be a criminal offence, so speak to your council's conservation officer before anything starts. Even if it isn't listed, a conservation area can affect what you can do to the outside.

When to get a survey

Get a proper damp survey if the damp keeps coming back after previous treatment, if you're buying a stone property and the survey or valuation mentions damp, or if someone has quoted for injection and full replastering after a quick look with a moisture meter. On solid stone walls, meter readings alone can be very misleading, so what matters is someone tracing the actual source of the water.

Common questions

Does a stone cottage need a damp-proof course?

Not necessarily. Many solid stone houses were built without one and stay dry as long as the walls can breathe and water is kept away from them. Damp is more often caused by cement pointing or render, gypsum plaster, plastic paints, raised ground levels or leaking gutters than by the lack of a damp-proof course.

Why has my stone wall got damp after it was repointed?

If it was repointed in hard cement mortar, the joints can no longer let moisture out, so water stays in the stone and the wall gets wetter. Soft stone around cement joints can also start to crumble. Repointing in a suitable lime mortar usually helps the wall dry out again.

Can I use lime plaster to fix damp on a stone wall?

Lime plaster lets the wall dry out rather than trapping moisture, so it is usually the better choice on solid stone. But it won't fix the cause on its own. Sort out where the water is coming from first, such as gutters, ground levels or failed pointing, then replaster.
