Damp patch on a chimney breast? Why it happens and how to fix it

By Jeff Webb, PCA Qualified Technician in Damp & Timber · 17 November 2026

Short answer: a damp patch on a chimney breast is almost never rising damp, so a damp-proof course won't fix it. In most homes it comes from one of three things: rain getting into the stack or down the flue, condensation inside a flue that has been sealed up with no airflow, or salts from years of coal fires that soak moisture out of the air and keep the plaster damp. Find which one you have, fix that, and the patch stops coming back.

Why chimney breasts get damp

Most older houses across Newcastle, Gateshead, Sunderland and Durham were built with coal fires in nearly every room. Since then, many fireplaces have been bricked up, boarded over or plastered flush, and plenty of stacks have been capped or left to weather. The chimney is still there, it's still exposed to wind and rain at the top, and the masonry inside has had decades of soot through it. That combination is why we see so many stained, bubbling or flaky chimney breasts once the heating goes on in winter.

The four usual causes

1. Rain getting in at the top

The stack is the most exposed bit of brickwork on the house. Water gets in through:

  • Open chimney pots with no cap or cowl, so rain falls straight down the flue
  • Cracked or missing flaunching (the mortar bed the pots sit in)
  • Worn pointing or porous bricks on the stack itself
  • Failed or lifted lead flashing where the stack meets the roof

Patches from rain usually get worse after a wet, windy spell and can show anywhere on the breast, including upstairs and in the loft. If yours behaves like that, our guide to damp patches after heavy rain covers the wider checks.

2. Condensation in a sealed-up flue

When a fireplace is blocked up and the pot is capped solid, the flue becomes a closed tube of cold masonry. Any moisture that gets in can't escape, so it condenses on the inside of the flue and soaks through to the plaster. The usual fix is to keep air moving: a vent in the blocked-up fireplace opening and a ventilated cap or cowl on the pot, rather than sealing it completely.

3. Salts from old coal fires

Burning coal and wood leaves deposits in the flue that contain salts. Over the years these migrate into the brickwork and plaster. Some of these salts are hygroscopic, meaning they pull moisture out of the air. The result is a patch that goes dark and damp when the weather is humid and dries back when it isn't, even if no water is getting in at all. You'll often see brown or yellow staining that comes back through fresh paint, and plaster that's soft or powdery.

This one catches people out because the leak may have been fixed years ago, yet the patch keeps returning. The salts stay in the plaster until it's taken off.

4. A shared stack in a terrace

In Tyneside flats and terraced streets, stacks are often shared with the neighbours. A defect on their side, or their open fire or a flue they've capped badly, can affect your breast. It's worth a polite word next door before assuming the problem is all yours.

What you can check yourself

  • Look at the stack from the street with binoculars or a phone zoom: missing pots or caps, cracked flaunching, plants growing out of it, gappy pointing, lifted lead
  • Note when the patch gets worse. After heavy rain points to water getting in. In muggy or humid weather, with no rain, points to salts or condensation
  • Check the height. A patch high up, upstairs or in the loft rules out rising damp
  • Look for a vent in the blocked-up fireplace. No vent and a solid cap on top is a common recipe for a damp flue
  • Look at the plaster. Brown tide-like staining that bleeds through paint, with soft or crumbly plaster, suggests salt contamination

Please don't go up on the roof yourself. A roofer or chimney specialist with proper access can check the stack safely.

How it's usually fixed

  • Stop water at the top first: repair flaunching, repoint the stack, renew flashing, and fit a rain cap or cowl to open pots
  • Ventilate disused flues: a vent at the bottom and a ventilated cap at the top, so air can pass through
  • Replace salt-contaminated plaster once the source is dealt with, using a plaster system designed to hold back salts. Just repainting or using a stain-block over salty plaster rarely lasts
  • Give it time to dry. Thick chimney masonry can take months to dry out after a long-running leak

What doesn't fix it: injecting a chemical damp-proof course into the base of the chimney breast. Rising damp isn't the cause, so injection is money spent on the wrong problem. If you've been quoted for it, read has my damp-proof course failed? before agreeing to anything.

If you're thinking of reopening a fireplace or fitting a stove, get the flue checked by a qualified installer or sweep first. That's a separate job from damp, but it's the moment to sort both.

When to get a survey

If the patch keeps coming back after repairs, you're not sure whether it's rain, condensation or salts, or someone is recommending damp-proof injection for a chimney breast, a survey is worth having before you spend money. We look at the stack, take moisture readings across the breast and nearby walls, and give you a written report on the cause and what it needs.

Common questions

Is a damp chimney breast rising damp?

Almost never. Damp on a chimney breast usually comes from rain getting into the stack, condensation in a sealed flue, or salts left in the plaster by years of coal fires. A damp-proof course won't fix any of those.

Should I cap my chimney if I don't use the fireplace?

Capping stops rain falling down the flue, but it should be a ventilated cap or cowl, with a vent fitted in the blocked-up fireplace below. Sealing a flue completely at both ends can trap moisture and cause condensation.

Why does the brown stain come back through new paint?

Usually because the plaster is contaminated with salts and soot deposits from old fires. These pull in moisture from the air and bleed through paint. The lasting fix is to stop any water getting in, then replace the affected plaster with a salt-resistant system.
