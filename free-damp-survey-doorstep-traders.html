Free damp survey on your doorstep? How to avoid a damp-proofing rip-off

By Jeff Webb, PCA Qualified Technician in Damp & Timber · 28 November 2026

Short answer: be very wary of anyone who knocks on your door, rings out of the blue or pushes a leaflet through offering a "free damp survey". The word "free" isn't the problem: plenty of local firms, us included, offer a free first inspection when you ask for one. The problem is the unsolicited visit, the instant diagnosis of rising damp and the pressure to sign for injection and replastering, when the real cause is often condensation, a leak or something cheap to fix. Never sign on the day, get a second opinion if you're unsure, and remember that if you do agree a contract in your home worth more than £42 you have 14 days to cancel.

Why this comes up every winter

As soon as the heating goes on in Newcastle, Sunderland and Durham, mould and damp patches appear, and so do the cold callers. Older North East homes, from Tyneside flats to colliery terraces, give a salesperson plenty of stains to point at.

The Property Care Association has warned homeowners about doorstep traders using "free survey" offers to get through the door, and notes that free is unlikely to stay free for long. Most damp firms are honest. What to watch for is a sales approach that skips finding the cause and goes straight to selling a treatment.

Red flags to watch for

  • Unsolicited contact. A knock on the door, a cold call or a leaflet saying they're "working in your area".
  • A diagnosis in minutes. A quick sweep with a moisture meter and a confident "that's rising damp". Meters are useful, but on their own they can't prove rising damp. I explain why in why a damp meter reading can mislead you.
  • Rising damp upstairs, or on every wall. Rising damp comes from the ground. Mould on bedroom ceilings and window reveals is almost always condensation.
  • No look outside. A proper survey checks ground levels, gutters, downpipes, pointing, render and air bricks. If they never stepped outside, they haven't looked for the cause.
  • Pressure to sign today. "Today-only" discounts, a deposit taken on the spot, or warnings that the house is about to "fall down" or "fail its mortgage".
  • Nothing explained. Just a quote with a price, and no clear explanation of what was found, where, and why it's happening.
  • A big guarantee instead of a diagnosis. A long guarantee is only as good as the firm behind it and what it actually covers. See what a damp-proof guarantee really covers.

Your right to cancel a doorstep contract

This is the bit most people don't know. Under consumer law, if you agree a contract in your own home (or anywhere away from the trader's business premises) for goods or services worth more than £42, you normally have 14 days to cancel, and you don't have to give a reason.

  • The trader must tell you about that right in writing, on paper or in a form you can keep, and give you a cancellation form.
  • If they don't tell you about your right to cancel, the cancellation period can be extended by up to 12 months.
  • Failing to give that information is a criminal offence for the trader.
  • If you ask them to start work inside the 14 days and then cancel, you may still have to pay a reasonable amount for work already done, so don't be rushed into letting them start.

If a trader refuses to accept a cancellation or you think you've been mis-sold, contact the Citizens Advice consumer helpline on 0808 223 1133. They pass reports on to Trading Standards. The official guidance is on GOV.UK under door to door sales.

I'm not a lawyer, and this is a plain-English summary. If you're in a dispute over a contract, get advice from Citizens Advice or a solicitor.

What a proper damp survey looks like

A real survey is about finding the cause, not selling a product. You should expect:

  • A look at the outside of the property as well as the inside
  • Questions about how the house is used: heating, drying washing, extractor fans
  • Readings taken in a pattern, so the surveyor can see where moisture is and how it's spread, not just a single beep
  • A check for things that commonly get mistaken for rising damp: bridged damp-proof courses, high ground or paths, leaking pipes, failed pointing and condensation
  • A plain explanation of what was found, what's causing it and what's recommended, including any cheaper fixes that should be tried first, and a written report if you need one (for example when buying or for a landlord)
  • Clear information about who they are and their qualifications

Often the honest answer is not chemical injection at all. Plenty of "failed" damp-proof courses are simply bridged, which I cover in has my damp-proof course failed?

Before you agree to any damp work

  • Don't sign on the day. Take the quote and the report away and read them.
  • Ask exactly what's causing the damp and how they know.
  • If you're unsure, get a second opinion from another firm before committing.
  • Check qualifications. The Property Care Association runs qualifications for damp and timber surveyors, and you can ask to see them.
  • Get everything in writing, including what any guarantee covers and who backs it.
  • If you're vulnerable or elderly, or helping a parent, never let someone start work the same day they knocked.

When to get a survey

If you've been told you have rising damp and you're not sure, if you've had a big quote after a free visit, or if you're buying a house and the report mentions damp, a survey with proper readings and a written report will tell you what's really going on before you spend money. Common questions

Are free damp surveys a scam?

A free inspection you've booked yourself from a local firm can be a sensible first step. What matters is how it's done: whether they look outside as well as in, explain the cause rather than just quoting for treatment, and give you time to think. Be far more cautious about a "free survey" from someone who turned up uninvited.

Can I cancel damp-proofing work I agreed to at home?

Usually yes. If you agreed the contract in your home and it's worth more than £42, you normally have 14 days to cancel without giving a reason. The trader must tell you this in writing, and if they don't, the cancellation period can be extended by up to 12 months.

How do I know if it's really rising damp?

Genuine rising damp is confined to ground-floor walls, usually shows a tide mark, salts and damaged plaster low down, and is there all year round. A proper diagnosis also rules out leaks, high ground, a bridged damp-proof course and condensation, which a quick meter reading can't do.
