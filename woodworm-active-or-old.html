Woodworm holes: is it active, or just old damage?

By Jeff Webb, PCA Qualified Technician in Damp & Timber · 12 November 2026

Short answer: most woodworm holes you find in North East homes are old, and the beetles that made them died out years ago. Small round holes on their own don't prove anything is still alive. Signs of active woodworm are fresh, clean-edged holes with pale wood inside, little piles of fine powder (frass) below the timber, and damp or soft wood. If you only see dark, dusty old holes in dry timber, you may not need any treatment at all.

Why it matters

Woodworm turns up all the time on homebuyer surveys and in lofts, under floorboards and in cellars across Newcastle, Sunderland, Durham and the rest of the region. Plenty of older Tyneside flats, colliery terraces and stone cottages have holes in their joists, rafters or floorboards. The question that actually matters isn't "is there woodworm?" but "is it still active, and is the timber still sound?" Spraying the whole house for an infestation that finished decades ago is money down the drain.

What woodworm actually is

"Woodworm" is the larva of a wood-boring beetle. The larvae tunnel inside timber, sometimes for several years, then turn into adult beetles and chew their way out, leaving the round exit holes you can see. By the time you spot a hole, that beetle has already gone.

In ordinary UK houses, the one we find by far the most is the common furniture beetle. Its exit holes are small and round, roughly 1 to 2mm across, and it attacks softwood joists, floorboards and roof timbers as well as old furniture. Other beetles exist, such as the deathwatch beetle, which leaves bigger holes and is usually found in old oak alongside fungal decay, but they're far less common in a typical North East home.

Signs the woodworm is active

  • Fresh exit holes: sharp, clean edges and pale, bright wood inside, rather than dark and dusty
  • Frass: fine, gritty, light-coloured powder in little piles under the timber or spilling out of holes
  • New holes appearing: holes you're sure weren't there last year
  • Adult beetles: small brown beetles near windowsills or on timber, usually in late spring and summer when they emerge
  • Damp timber: woodworm larvae do much better in damp wood, so timber near a leak, in a damp cellar or under a wet floor is a far bigger worry
  • Weak or crumbly wood: edges of boards that break away easily, or a sharp tool going in too easily

Signs it's old, inactive damage

  • Holes that are dark inside and look the same colour as the surrounding wood
  • Dust and cobwebs in or over the holes
  • No fresh frass, even after a summer has passed
  • Dry timber in a heated, ventilated house
  • Old treatment marks or labels, or a previous guarantee that came with the house

Modern central heating dries out house timbers over time, and dry timber is much less attractive to woodworm larvae. That's one of the main reasons so many infestations in older properties simply die out on their own.

A simple check you can do yourself

If you're not sure, try this. Clean off any old dust from the area, then either lay a sheet of dark paper underneath the timber, or rub a little furniture wax or masking over a patch of existing holes. Leave it through the spring and summer. If fresh powder appears on the paper or new holes break through the wax, something is still alive in there. If nothing changes, the holes are very likely historic.

It's a slow test, so if you're buying a house or need an answer quickly, a timber survey is the faster route.

When woodworm does need treating

Where there is genuine active infestation, the usual approach is:

  • Fix the moisture first. Leaking roofs, gutters, damp cellars and poor sub-floor ventilation keep timber wet. Without sorting that, any treatment is fighting the cause.
  • Treat only the affected timbers. A suitable insecticide treatment applied to the timbers that are actually affected, not a blanket spray of every bit of wood in the house.
  • Replace timber that's lost its strength. If a joist or rafter has been badly weakened, it needs repairing or replacing, and that's a structural job, not a spray job.

Woodworm and damp often go together. If the timber is soft and damp, it's also worth ruling out rot. Our guide to dry rot and wet rot explains how to tell them apart.

Woodworm on a homebuyer survey or mortgage valuation

Surveyors often note "evidence of wood-boring insect infestation" and recommend a specialist inspection. That doesn't mean the house is riddled. It usually means the surveyor saw holes but couldn't say whether they're active. Don't panic and don't pull out of the sale, but do get it checked properly before exchange so you know whether you're looking at nothing, a small treatment, or some timber repairs. Our article on damp on a homebuyer survey covers how to handle survey findings more generally.

Watch out for scare tactics

  • Being told the whole house needs spraying because of a few old holes in the loft
  • No moisture readings taken from the affected timber
  • No explanation of why the infestation is considered active
  • A treatment quote with no mention of fixing the damp that may be behind it
  • Pressure to sign up on the day

A good timber report should tell you whether it's active, which timbers are affected, whether any have lost strength, and what's causing the moisture, in plain English.

When to get a survey

Get a timber survey if you've found fresh frass or new holes, if timber feels soft or damp, if you're buying and the survey mentions woodworm, or if you've been given a big treatment quote and want an independent view first.

Common questions

Do old woodworm holes mean I have woodworm now?

Not necessarily. Exit holes are left by beetles that have already left the wood, and many infestations in older homes died out years ago. Fresh, clean holes, new powder and damp timber are the signs that it may still be active.

Does woodworm need treating straight away?

Only if it's active. Old, inactive holes in dry, sound timber usually need no treatment. If it is active, the moisture problem behind it should be fixed and only the affected timbers treated or repaired.

Is woodworm connected to damp?

Yes. Woodworm larvae do much better in damp timber, which is why active infestations are often found near leaks, in damp cellars and under poorly ventilated floors. Keeping timber dry is one of the best long-term protections.
