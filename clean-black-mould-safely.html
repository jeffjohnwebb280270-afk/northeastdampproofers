How to clean black mould safely (and why bleach isn't the fix)

By Jeff Webb, PCA Qualified Technician in Damp & Timber · 24 November 2026

Short answer: a small patch of black mould on a window reveal, sealant or a corner of the ceiling is usually fine to clean yourself, as long as you protect yourself, keep the room ventilated, use a proper mould remover rather than neat bleach, and never dry-brush or vacuum it. But cleaning only removes what you can see. If you don't deal with the moisture that caused it, it will be back within weeks, and a large area, mould that keeps returning, or anyone vulnerable in the house is a reason to get it looked at properly.

Why it's everywhere at this time of year

Late November in Newcastle, Gateshead, Sunderland and Durham: the heating's on, windows stay shut and washing is drying on radiators. That moist air condenses on the coldest surfaces, such as window reveals, outside-wall corners, behind wardrobes and bathroom ceilings, and black mould follows. If you're not sure whether yours is condensation or something else, read black mould in winter: condensation or rising damp? first.

Should you clean it yourself?

For a small, surface patch on a hard surface, yes, most people can. The government's guidance on damp and mould is clear that "everyone is vulnerable to the health impacts of damp and mould", and that people with health conditions, children and older adults are at greater risk. So leave the cleaning to someone else, or get help, if the person doing it has:

  • asthma, allergies, COPD or another lung condition
  • a weakened immune system
  • a pregnancy, or has recently given birth

And don't tackle it yourself if the mould covers a large area such as most of a wall or ceiling, has gone into plasterboard, carpet or soft furnishings, or keeps coming back however often you clean it. That's a sign of a bigger moisture problem that needs finding.

How to clean a small patch safely

  1. Protect yourself. Rubber gloves, goggles and a proper fitted dust mask (FFP2 or better), not a thin paper one. Wear old clothes and wash them straight afterwards.
  2. Close the door, open the window. Keeping the door shut stops spores drifting round the rest of the house; the open window gives them somewhere to go.
  3. Don't brush or vacuum dry mould. It throws spores into the air, which is exactly what you're trying to avoid. Dampen it first with the cleaner.
  4. Use a purpose-made mould remover. Follow the instructions on the bottle, including how long to leave it on. For larger washable areas, a fungicidal wash that carries an HSE approval number is the better choice.
  5. Wipe with disposable cloths. Use a fresh part of the cloth each time so you're not smearing it around, then bag the cloths and bin them.
  6. Dry the surface. Leaving it wet gives the next lot of mould a head start. A towel, a bit of heat and air movement all help.

Never mix bleach with other cleaning products, especially anything containing ammonia or acid-based descalers: the combination gives off dangerous gases.

Why bleach isn't the answer

Bleach makes mould vanish from view on a shiny tile. The trouble is porous surfaces like plaster, painted walls, grout and silicone sealant: bleach takes the colour out, so the wall looks clean, but mould can still be living in the surface underneath. It's also mostly water, so you're adding moisture to a surface that already had too much. In our experience that's a big part of why people tell us "I bleached it and it was back in a fortnight".

Use a product made for the job, and accept that some things can't be cleaned:

  • Silicone sealant with black spots through it: cut it out, clean and dry the joint, and reseal.
  • Plasterboard or wallpaper with mould growing through it: it usually needs replacing, and stripping it releases a lot of spores, so it's better done by someone with the right kit.
  • Carpets, curtains and soft furnishings: wash what you can; badly affected items may need throwing away.

Don't paint over it

Anti-mould paint only belongs on a clean, dry wall once the cause is sorted. Painted over live mould, or over damp from a leak, it just hides a problem that's still there.

Stopping it coming back

As the government guidance puts it, "simply removing surface mould will not prevent the damp and mould from reappearing". Cleaning buys you time; this is what actually fixes it for condensation mould:

  • run extractor fans when cooking and showering, and for a while afterwards
  • keep trickle vents open, even in winter
  • dry washing outside or in a closed room with the window open, not on radiators in a bedroom
  • keep the heating low and steady rather than off all day and blasting at night
  • pull wardrobes and beds a few centimetres off outside walls

A dehumidifier can help in a flat that's hard to ventilate (see will a dehumidifier fix damp?). If the mould comes back despite all of that, the house may need better ventilation, such as continuous extract fans or positive input ventilation, or cold spots dealing with.

If you rent

Clean small patches to protect your health, but still report the mould to your landlord in writing, with dates and photos. Recurring mould is often down to the building, not how you live. See our guides for private tenants and on Awaab's Law in social housing.

When to get a survey

Get the mould looked at by someone who'll find the cause if:

  • it's back within a few weeks of cleaning
  • it covers a large area, or it's on one wall only and gets worse after rain
  • it's low down on a ground-floor wall with tide marks or salts
  • there's a musty smell but you can't find where it's coming from
  • you're a landlord and a tenant has reported it

Common questions

Is bleach good for killing black mould?

Not on most household surfaces. Bleach removes the colour, so mould looks gone, but on porous surfaces like plaster, grout and sealant it can still be there underneath, and the water in bleach adds moisture. A purpose-made mould remover used as directed is a better choice, and bleach must never be mixed with other cleaners.

Can I paint over black mould?

No. Clean it off, let the surface dry completely and fix the cause first. Anti-mould paint is only worth using on a clean, dry wall; painted straight over live mould, it just hides it until it comes back through.

How much mould is too much to clean myself?

There's no exact cut-off, but a small surface patch on a hard surface is usually fine for a healthy adult to clean with the right precautions. Mould over a large area such as a wall or ceiling, mould inside plasterboard or carpet, mould that keeps coming back, or anyone with asthma or a weakened immune system in the house all point to getting professional help and finding the cause.
