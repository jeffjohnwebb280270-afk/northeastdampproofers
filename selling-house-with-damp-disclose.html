Selling a house with damp: do you have to tell the buyer?

By Jeff Webb, PCA Qualified Technician in Damp & Timber · 25 November 2026

Short answer: you don't have to volunteer every fault in your house, but you must answer the seller's property information form (the TA6) truthfully, and if you know about damp, a past leak or old damp-proofing work, you shouldn't hide it or say "no" when the honest answer is "yes" or "not known". A misleading answer can let the buyer claim compensation from you after completion. In most cases the better move is to find out what the damp actually is before you list, fix the cause if it's cheap, and hand over the paperwork.

What the law actually expects of a seller

House sales in England still work on the old principle of "buyer beware". The buyer is expected to do their own checks: a survey, searches and questions through their solicitor. Silence on its own isn't normally treated as misrepresentation.

The catch is the TA6 property information form, which almost every seller fills in through their solicitor. The Law Society's notes for the form are plain about it:

  • Answer truthfully, completely and as accurately as you can from your own knowledge.
  • If you don't know an answer, say so. Don't guess.
  • Buyers can rely on what you write, and if you give misleading information, the buyer may be able to claim compensation after completion.
  • If something changes after you've filled it in, tell your solicitor straight away.

The current version is the 6th edition, which the Home Owners Alliance says replaced the earlier editions on 30 March 2026. If you're selling now, that's almost certainly the one you'll be sent.

Estate agents also have their own duty under consumer protection law not to leave out material information they know about. If you've told your agent about a damp problem, they can't just keep quiet about it to buyers.

This is general information, not legal advice. Your conveyancing solicitor is the right person to check any specific answer with.

Where damp comes up on the form

Damp isn't one tick box. It tends to come up in several places:

  • Guarantees and warranties: you're asked about unexpired guarantees, which specifically include damp-proofing and timber treatment, whether any claims have been made, and whether you know of anything that could breach the terms.
  • Flooding: whether the property has flooded and what type of flooding it was (surface water, groundwater, river, coastal or sewer).
  • Alterations and building work: what work has been done and whether it had the right consents.
  • Receipts and paperwork: the notes suggest gathering paperwork for work such as damp proofing so you can pass it on.

Buyers' solicitors often add extra written questions too, especially if the buyer's survey has flagged damp. Those answers count just as much as the form itself.

Things sellers get wrong

  • Painting over it before viewings. Fresh paint over a damp patch, or a new skirting board over a rotten one, doesn't stop a surveyor's meter. If you're later asked directly and say there's no damp, that's where trouble starts.
  • Answering "no" when you mean "not sure". If you've seen a damp patch and never found out why, "not known" or a short honest note is safer than a flat "no".
  • Losing the guarantee. A transferable damp-proofing or timber guarantee is worth having in the pack, but buyers will want the paperwork, not a memory of "a company did it in the nineties". Check whether the firm still exists and whether the guarantee is insurance-backed.
  • Paying for a big fix you don't need. Some sellers panic and buy an injected damp-proof course to "get it sorted" before listing. If the real cause was a leaking gutter, bridged damp-proof course or condensation, it won't work, and the buyer's surveyor may still find damp.

Should you fix damp before you sell?

It depends on what it is. Across Newcastle, Sunderland, Durham and the rest of the region, a lot of damp in older terraces, Tyneside flats and stone cottages turns out to have a simple cause: a blocked or leaking gutter, high ground or a path against the wall, cracked render, or winter condensation. Those are often cheap to sort and worth doing before you list.

Bigger problems, like genuine rising damp, a damp cellar or timber decay in a floor, are a judgement call. You can:

  • Fix it first and pass on the report, invoice and any guarantee, so the buyer's surveyor has an answer when they find the old evidence.
  • Sell as it is and give the buyer an independent survey report with the cause and a written scope of work, so they can price it in rather than imagine the worst.

Either way, a clear written diagnosis usually helps. A vague "damp noted, further investigation recommended" on a buyer's survey is often what makes a sale wobble, because nobody knows how big the problem is. What to have ready

  • Any damp-proofing or timber treatment guarantee, with the company's details
  • Invoices for repairs: roof, gutters, pointing, render, plumbing leaks
  • Any previous damp or timber survey reports
  • Insurance claim details if you've had a leak or flood
  • A short honest note of anything you've seen and what was done about it

When to get a survey

It's worth getting a damp survey before you list if you've seen damp patches, tide marks, salts or mould and never found the cause; if old injection holes or replastering suggest previous treatment and you've no paperwork; or if a buyer's survey has already flagged damp and you want an independent second view before you agree a price reduction. Common questions

Do I have to tell buyers about damp when selling my house?

You don't have to volunteer every defect, but you must answer the TA6 property information form and any solicitor's questions truthfully. If you know about damp, past leaks or damp-proofing work, don't deny it. If you're unsure, say you don't know. A misleading answer can lead to a compensation claim after completion.

What if I had damp treated years ago?

Say so, and pass on the guarantee and paperwork if you have them. The form asks about guarantees for damp-proofing and timber treatment. A buyer's surveyor will often spot old injection holes anyway, so a clear record of what was done helps rather than hurts.

Is it worth fixing damp before selling?

Often yes if the cause is simple, such as a leaking gutter, high ground against the wall or condensation. For bigger jobs, a written survey with the cause and a scope of work lets buyers price it in, which can be better than rushing into expensive treatment that may not be needed.
