Timber & damp advice

Should you block air bricks in winter? Why it can rot your floor

By Jeff Webb, PCA Qualified Technician in Damp & Timber · 18 November 2026

Short answer: no. If your house has a suspended timber ground floor, the air bricks low down on the outside walls are what keep the space under the floorboards dry. Block them to stop a draught and the void goes stale and damp, the joists and boards soak up moisture, and over time that can lead to wet rot, dry rot and woodworm. The draught is real, but the fix belongs inside the room, not over the air brick.

What air bricks actually do

A lot of older North East homes, from Victorian Tyneside flats and colliery terraces to 1930s semis, were built with timber floors sitting on joists over a shallow void. The ground under that void is often bare earth or a thin layer of concrete, and it gives off moisture all year round.

Air bricks set into the outside walls, usually on at least two opposite sides, let air flow straight through that void and carry the moisture away. Without that cross-flow, the air under the floor gets damp and stays damp, and the timber sitting in it slowly takes on water.

For new homes, NHBC guidance asks for a ventilated void of at least 150mm under the floor, with vents on at least two opposite external walls and an opening area of 1,500mm² per metre run of wall or 500mm² per square metre of floor, whichever is greater. Older houses weren't built to that standard, but the principle is the same: air in one side, air out the other.

Why people block them in winter

It's an understandable thing to do. When the wind comes off the North Sea, cold air can whistle up through gaps between floorboards and under skirting, and the air brick looks like the obvious culprit. Common things we see:

  • Air bricks stuffed with rags, newspaper or expanding foam
  • Covers or tape stuck over them from outside
  • Vents hidden behind a new path, patio, decking or raised flower bed
  • Vents rendered or pointed over during outside work
  • Air bricks blocked inside the void by rubble or old building debris
  • An extension built against the wall, cutting off the cross-flow

The last few aren't always deliberate, but the effect on the floor is the same.

What happens when the void can't breathe

Timber is happy as long as it stays reasonably dry. Wood-rotting fungi need wet timber to get going, and the ends of joists built into damp walls are usually the first place to suffer. Signs a subfloor isn't getting enough air include:

  • A musty, earthy smell in ground-floor rooms, often worse with the heating on
  • Floorboards that feel soft, springy or bouncy, especially near outside walls
  • Skirting boards that are stained, swollen or crumbling
  • Mould on the underside of carpets or underlay
  • Gaps opening up as boards shrink or cup
  • Fine dust or small holes in the timber, which may point to woodworm, which favours damp wood

A damp subfloor can also push moisture up into the room, adding to condensation on cold walls. If you're not sure which kind of rot you might be looking at, our guide to telling dry rot from wet rot explains the differences.

Better ways to stop the draught

You can have a warm room and a ventilated floor. The trick is to seal the room off from the void, not seal the void off from the outside.

  • Fill gaps between floorboards with a flexible filler or purpose-made strips
  • Seal the gap under skirting boards with a suitable sealant or draught-proofing strip
  • Lay a good underlay under carpets, or rugs on bare boards
  • Draught-proof doors and letterboxes, which are often the bigger source of cold air
  • Fit an air brick cover designed to cut wind-driven draughts while still letting air through, rather than a solid blank
  • If you're insulating under the floor, have it done so the void stays ventilated below the insulation

One important safety point

Not every vent is for the floor. Some homes have vents that supply air to a room with a gas fire, an open fire or an older boiler. Those must never be blocked, because the appliance needs that air to burn safely. If you're not sure what a vent is for, leave it open and ask a Gas Safe registered engineer.

What you can check yourself

  • Walk round the outside and find your air bricks, usually just above ground level and below the damp-proof course
  • Check each one is clear: no foam, rags, plants, soil or paving over it
  • Make sure ground levels and paths sit well below them; a raised path can also bridge the damp-proof course, as we cover in has my damp-proof course failed?
  • Look for walls with no air bricks at all, or a side cut off by an extension
  • Inside, press down on boards near outside walls and sniff for mustiness
  • If you can safely lift a board or open a hatch, look for wet soil, standing water, debris or white fungal strands

Unblocking an air brick is usually a five-minute job. Don't be surprised if the room feels a bit cooler afterwards; deal with that from the inside using the steps above.

When to get a survey

If boards are soft, there's a persistent musty smell, or you've found an air brick that's been blocked for years, it's worth having the floor looked at before damage spreads. Catching damp timber early often means drying the void out and repairing a few joist ends, rather than replacing a whole floor.

Buyers should take note too. A blocked or buried air brick is easy to miss on a viewing, and a homebuyer survey may only flag it as a possible concern.

Common questions

Is it OK to block air bricks in winter?

Not if they ventilate a suspended timber floor. Blocking them traps moisture under the floor and can lead to damp joists, rot and woodworm. Stop draughts from inside the room instead, by sealing gaps between floorboards and under skirting boards.

How many air bricks does my house need?

It depends on the size of the floor and the layout of the walls. What matters is that air can flow right through the void, which usually means clear vents on at least two opposite sides. Houses with a side blocked by an extension sometimes need extra ventilation added.

Can I fit covers to my air bricks?

Fit only covers designed to let air through, such as louvred or baffled types, and never solid blanks. Never cover a vent that supplies air to a gas fire, open fire or boiler.
