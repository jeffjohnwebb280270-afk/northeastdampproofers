Dry rot or wet rot? How to tell the difference

By Jeff Webb, PCA Qualified Technician in Damp & Timber · 9 November 2026

Short answer: both dry rot and wet rot are fungi that eat damp timber, and both start because wood has stayed wet for too long. The difference is how far they travel. Wet rot stays where the water is: fix the leak, dry the timber out and replace what's lost. Dry rot can spread well beyond the wet spot, sending strands across and through brickwork to reach new timber, so it needs a more careful survey. Either way, the moisture source is the real problem, and no chemical replaces fixing it.

Why this question comes up in the North East

A lot of homes across Newcastle, Gateshead, Sunderland and Durham have suspended timber ground floors, timber lintels, sash windows and joists bedded into solid brick or stone walls. Tyneside flats, colliery terraces and older stone cottages often have all of these. When a gutter overflows, a pipe weeps under the floor, or airbricks get blocked or buried, that timber can sit wet for months without anyone noticing. Rot is usually the moment people find out.

The name "dry rot" confuses people. It doesn't grow in dry wood. It gets its name from what it leaves behind: timber that looks dry, brittle and crumbly once the fungus has finished with it.

Signs it's wet rot

  • Decay confined to the area that's obviously wet: a window sill, the bottom of a door frame, a joist end under a leak
  • Timber that feels soft and spongy, often darker in colour
  • Paint that looks fine on the surface but the wood underneath gives way when pressed
  • Sometimes fine, dark, thread-like strands on the timber surface, but they don't spread far from the wet area
  • No growth over the brickwork or plaster around it

Wet rot is the more common of the two. It's annoying, but because it stops when the wood dries out, the repair is usually limited to the timber that's actually been damaged.

Signs it's dry rot

  • White, cotton-wool-like growth in damp, still, unventilated spots, such as under floors or behind skirting boards
  • A grey or lilac-tinged skin over the growth as it matures
  • Strands running across plaster or brickwork, sometimes a fair distance from the timber
  • Timber that has shrunk and split into rough cubes, both along and across the grain, and crumbles in your fingers
  • A flat, pancake-like fruiting body, with a rusty red-brown dust of spores settling around it
  • A musty, mushroom-type smell in an enclosed space

The strands are what make dry rot different. They can grow through and across masonry to reach fresh timber elsewhere, which is why a small patch you can see may be part of something bigger behind the plaster or under the floor.

What both have in common: moisture

Wood-rotting fungi need water. Timber kept below about 20 per cent moisture content is at little risk of decay, and drying the building out is the main way to stop both types. That means finding and fixing the source first:

  • Leaking or overflowing gutters and downpipes
  • Plumbing leaks under floors or in walls
  • Blocked, buried or missing airbricks starving the underfloor void of ventilation
  • Ground or path levels outside built up above the damp-proof course
  • Failed pointing, cracked render or defective flashings letting rain in
  • Rising or penetrating damp in walls where joist ends and lintels sit

How each is usually repaired

Wet rot: fix the water, let things dry, cut out and replace the decayed timber, and treat or use pre-treated replacement timber where it will stay at risk. Sound timber nearby is normally kept.

Dry rot: the moisture source still comes first. After that, the surveyor needs to trace how far the growth has travelled, which can mean lifting boards or removing some plaster. Decayed timber is removed and replaced, and treatment may be used to stop the fungus reaching new timber while the structure dries. Drying out heavy masonry walls can take a long time, so ventilation and patience matter as much as anything sprayed on.

Older guidance often led to very aggressive work: stripping large areas of plaster and cutting out lots of sound timber "just in case". Current good practice puts much more weight on getting and keeping the building dry. Be cautious of anyone who wants to rip out half a room on a first visit without explaining where the water is coming from.

Cowboy warning signs

  • Calling everything "dry rot" without showing you the evidence
  • Quoting for chemicals alone, with nothing about the leak or ventilation that caused it
  • No written report, no moisture readings and no photos
  • Pressure to sign on the day

When to get a survey

Get a timber survey if you find white growth or rusty dust under a floor, if boards feel springy or have dropped, if a homebuyer survey mentions rot or recommends further investigation, or if you're a landlord with a tenant reporting a damp, musty smell. A proper inspection identifies which fungus it is, how far it has gone and, most importantly, where the moisture is coming from.

Common questions

Is dry rot worse than wet rot?

It is usually more serious, because dry rot can spread through masonry to reach timber away from the original wet area, while wet rot stays where the timber is wet. Both can weaken structural timber if they are left, and both stop once the timber is dried out and kept dry.

Can I treat rot myself?

Small, clearly local wet rot, such as a rotten section of window sill with an obvious cause, is often a joinery repair. Anything under floors, in joists or lintels, or any sign of white growth or red-brown spore dust is worth having surveyed, because the damage you can see may not be all of it.

Does a damp-proof course stop dry rot?

Not on its own. Dry rot starts wherever timber stays wet, and that is often from leaks, poor underfloor ventilation or rain getting in rather than rising damp. The moisture source has to be found and fixed, whatever it turns out to be.
