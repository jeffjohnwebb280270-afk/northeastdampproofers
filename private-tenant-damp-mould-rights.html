Private tenant with damp and mould? Your rights if the landlord won't fix it

By Jeff Webb, PCA Qualified Technician in Damp & Timber · 13 November 2026

Short answer: if you rent privately in England and your landlord won't deal with damp and mould, you're not stuck. Your landlord already has a legal duty to keep the home fit to live in, and damp and mould growth is one of the hazards that counts. Report it in writing, keep evidence, and if nothing happens within a reasonable time, contact your council's private housing team, who can inspect and order repairs. Awaab's Law, with its fixed deadlines, doesn't apply to private rentals yet. The government is consulting on extending it, but no start date has been set.

What the law already says

Two pieces of law do most of the work for private tenants in England today:

  • The Homes (Fitness for Human Habitation) Act 2018. Your landlord must make sure the home is fit to live in at the start of the tenancy and throughout it. Damp and mould growth is on the list of hazards used to judge that. If it isn't fit, you can take your landlord to court yourself, and the court can order repairs and compensation.
  • The Housing Health and Safety Rating System (HHSRS), under the Housing Act 2004. This is what council environmental health officers use when they inspect. If they find a serious (Category 1) hazard, the council must act. For less serious (Category 2) hazards, it can act. That can mean an improvement notice telling the landlord what to fix and by when.

Since 1 May 2026, private landlords in England can no longer serve section 21 "no-fault" eviction notices. That removes one of the main reasons tenants were afraid to complain. Notices that were validly served before that date can still be used, so take advice if you've had one.

Does Awaab's Law apply to private renters?

Not yet. Awaab's Law has applied to social housing (council and housing association homes) since 27 October 2025. The Renters' Rights Act 2025 gives the government the power to extend it to private tenancies, and a consultation on how to do that is open until 18 December 2026. The earliest start date being discussed is 2027, but nothing is confirmed. If you see a firm date quoted somewhere, treat it with caution. There's more on the social housing side in our article on Awaab's Law phase two.

Is it "your lifestyle" or the building?

This is where most disputes start. Some landlords put every bit of mould down to tenants drying washing indoors. Sometimes that's part of it. Often it isn't the whole story. The law says a landlord may not have to fix a problem a tenant caused by acting irresponsibly, but condensation in a cold, badly ventilated home isn't usually that.

From what I see on surveys around Newcastle, Gateshead, Sunderland and Durham, the building is often part of the problem:

  • Extractor fans that don't work, are too weak, or were never fitted in the kitchen or bathroom
  • Windows with no trickle vents, or vents painted shut
  • Cold solid walls in older terraces and Tyneside flats, with mould on the outside walls and corners
  • Heating that's too expensive to run or doesn't heat the room properly
  • Penetrating damp from blocked gutters, cracked render or poor pointing, which then looks like condensation inside
  • Leaks from a flat above, or plumbing hidden in walls and floors

That said, being honest about your own habits helps your case. Use the fans, keep trickle vents open, dry washing in one ventilated room with the door shut where you can, and leave a gap behind furniture on outside walls. If the mould still comes back, that points to the property. Our guide to black mould, condensation and rising damp explains how to tell the types apart.

Step by step: getting it fixed

  • Report it in writing. Email or text is fine if you keep a copy. Say where the damp or mould is, when it started, and that you're asking for it to be investigated and repaired.
  • Take dated photos. Photograph the mould, any water staining, broken fans or blocked vents. Take more every week or two so you can show it getting worse.
  • Keep a simple log. Note when you reported it, any replies, any visits, and any health effects, especially for children, older people or anyone with breathing problems.
  • Allow access. Your landlord usually needs to give at least 24 hours' notice for non-emergency visits. Letting them in shows you're being reasonable.
  • Give it a reasonable time. If nothing happens, contact your council's private rented housing or environmental health team and ask for an inspection.
  • Get advice before court. Shelter and Citizens Advice can help you decide whether a claim under the Homes Act is worth pursuing.

Don't stop paying rent to force a repair. It can put your tenancy at risk, even with the newer protections.

A note for landlords

If a tenant reports mould, the most useful thing you can do is find the cause before you argue about it. A bleach wash and a fresh coat of paint won't last a winter if a fan isn't pulling air or a gutter is overflowing down the wall. Fixing the cause now protects your tenant, your property and your position if the council ever gets involved, and it's the direction the law is heading anyway.

When to get a survey

An independent damp survey makes sense when the cause isn't obvious, when the landlord and tenant disagree about whether it's condensation or something in the building, or when mould keeps coming back after it's been treated. A good survey should take moisture readings, look at ventilation, heating, gutters and the outside walls, and give a written report of what's causing the damp and what will fix it.

If you're a tenant, ask your landlord to arrange the investigation first, as it's their responsibility. If you're a landlord, a written report gives you a clear plan and a record that you acted. Common questions

Can my landlord blame me for mould?

A landlord may not have to fix a problem a tenant caused by acting irresponsibly, but ordinary cooking, washing and breathing isn't that. If the home has broken fans, no ventilation, cold walls or water getting in, the landlord is responsible for dealing with the cause.

Does Awaab's Law apply to private landlords yet?

No. It applies to social housing in England. The Renters' Rights Act 2025 allows it to be extended to private rentals, and the government is consulting on that until 18 December 2026. No start date has been confirmed.

Who do I contact if my landlord ignores damp and mould?

Report it to your landlord in writing first. If it isn't dealt with in a reasonable time, contact your local council's private rented housing or environmental health team, who can inspect the home and order repairs. Shelter and Citizens Advice can also advise you.
