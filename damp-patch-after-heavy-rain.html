Damp patch after heavy rain? It's probably penetrating damp

By Jeff Webb, PCA Qualified Technician in Damp & Timber · 8 November 2026

Short answer: a damp patch that appears or darkens after heavy rain, then fades as the weather dries up, is almost always penetrating damp: rainwater getting in from outside through a defect in the roof, walls, gutters, chimney or windows. It isn't rising damp and it isn't condensation, so a damp-proof course or a dehumidifier won't fix it. The cure is to find where the water is getting in and repair that.

How to tell it's penetrating damp

Penetrating damp has a few tell-tale habits that set it apart from the other types:

  • It gets worse during or just after rain, especially wind-driven rain, and eases in dry spells
  • It can appear at any height: high on a wall, on a ceiling, around a window, or on a chimney breast
  • It's usually a defined patch rather than a band along the bottom of the wall
  • It's often on the wall that faces the prevailing weather
  • The plaster may have a brown or yellowish stain, and paint or wallpaper may bubble or peel
  • Upstairs ceilings and top-floor flats are common spots, which rules out rising damp straight away

Black mould spots spread across cold corners point to condensation instead. If you're not sure which you have, our guide to condensation versus rising damp explains the differences.

The usual culprits in North East homes

Most of the penetrating damp we see across Newcastle, Gateshead, Sunderland and Durham comes from a short list of defects. Work from the top of the building down:

  • Blocked or leaking gutters and downpipes. Autumn leaves and moss block gutters, water overflows and soaks the wall below. A cracked downpipe or a joint that's come apart can pour water straight onto the brickwork.
  • Roof and chimney defects. Slipped slates, failed lead flashing where the roof meets the chimney or a party wall, and a cracked chimney crown or missing cap all let water in. Damp on a chimney breast after rain is a classic sign.
  • Failed pointing. Eroded or cracked mortar joints let rain soak into solid brick and stone walls. On older stone cottages and colliery terraces, hard cement repointing over the original softer mortar can make things worse by trapping water in the stone.
  • Cracked or blown render. Render that has cracked lets water in behind it, and then holds that water against the wall instead of letting it dry out.
  • Windows and sills. Perished sealant around frames, sills with no drip groove underneath, or sills that slope back towards the wall can all channel water inside.
  • Cavity wall problems. In newer homes, mortar snots or debris lying across the cavity, or wall ties bridging it, can carry water from the outer leaf to the inner one.

Homes on the coast, from South Shields and Whitley Bay up through Northumberland, and anything on high, exposed ground, take a real battering from wind-driven rain, so small defects show up there sooner.

Tyneside flats and shared gutters

If you live in a Tyneside flat, the upper and lower flats often share the roof drainage, front wall and downpipes. Damp in the downstairs flat can be caused by a gutter or roof defect above the upstairs flat. Check your lease or title deeds to see who is responsible for which parts. Talking to your neighbour early usually saves a lot of hassle.

What you can check yourself

You can often narrow down the cause safely from the ground:

  • Stand outside during heavy rain and watch the gutters above the damp patch. Are they overflowing?
  • Look for water running down the face of the wall, or a downpipe that's leaking at a joint
  • Use binoculars to check the roof and chimney for slipped slates, gaps in the flashing or plants growing out of the stack
  • Look at the pointing and render on the affected wall for cracks or missing mortar
  • Check that window sills have a groove underneath so water drips off rather than running back to the wall
  • Note when the patch gets worse. If it's only in wind from one direction, that wall is your starting point

Don't go up ladders onto roofs yourself. Get a roofer or a gutter cleaner to do work at height.

What not to do

  • Don't just paint over it. Stain-blocking paint hides the mark for a while, but the water is still getting in and the wall stays wet behind it.
  • Don't let anyone sell you a damp-proof course for it. Injection damp-proofing is for rising damp at the base of a wall. It does nothing for water coming in higher up.
  • Don't seal a solid wall with the wrong product. Some masonry coatings help on the right wall, but sealing a wall that needs to breathe, or one that's already wet, can trap moisture inside.
  • Don't replaster before the leak is fixed. Fix the source, let the wall dry out, then deal with the plaster. Salts left behind by the water may mean some plaster needs replacing.

Renting? Who fixes it

For most tenancies in England of under seven years, section 11 of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985 makes the landlord responsible for keeping the structure and exterior in repair, including the roof, walls, gutters, drains and external pipes. Penetrating damp from those defects is therefore normally the landlord's job to fix. Tenants should report it in writing and keep photos and dates. If you rent from a council or housing association, our article on Awaab's Law explains the timescales social landlords must now meet for damp and mould.

When to get a survey

If you've cleared the gutters and checked the obvious and the patch keeps coming back, or you can't see where the water is coming from, it's time for a proper inspection. A damp survey uses moisture readings and a careful look inside and out to trace the water to its source, so you pay for the right repair rather than guessing.

Get one too if someone has quoted injection work for damp well above ground level, or if timber near the patch feels soft, as persistently wet masonry can lead to wet rot.

Common questions

Why does my wall only get damp when it rains?

Because water is getting in from outside. A patch that appears during or just after rain and fades in dry weather points to penetrating damp from a defect such as a blocked gutter, leaking downpipe, failed pointing, cracked render or a roof or chimney problem.

Will a damp-proof course fix damp that appears after rain?

No. A damp-proof course is designed to stop moisture rising from the ground at the base of a wall. Damp caused by rain getting in needs the external defect found and repaired, such as the gutter, roof, pointing or render.

Can penetrating damp cause mould?

Yes. A wall that keeps getting wet from outside is colder and damper than the rest of the room, so mould can grow on it. Cleaning the mould helps, but it will keep coming back until the water getting in is stopped.
