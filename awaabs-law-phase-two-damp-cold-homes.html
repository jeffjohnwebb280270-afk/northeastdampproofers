Awaab's Law phase two: what changes on 30 November

By Jeff Webb, PCA Qualified Technician in Damp & Timber · 6 November 2026

Short answer: from 30 November 2026, Awaab's Law is due to extend beyond damp, mould and emergencies to six more groups of hazard in social housing in England, including excess cold. The government described that date as subject to parliamentary approval when it announced it. Nothing changes for private tenants yet. For anyone living with a damp, cold home, the important point is that landlords will increasingly have to deal with the cause, not just the patch of mould.

What phase two adds

Damp and mould hazards, plus emergency hazards, have been covered since 27 October 2025. The government's guidance says phase two extends the law to these hazards where they present a significant risk of harm:

  • Excess cold and excess heat
  • Falls, including on the level, on stairs and between levels
  • Structural collapse
  • Fire and explosions
  • Electrical hazards
  • Domestic hygiene, including personal hygiene and food safety

A third phase, covering the remaining hazards in the Housing Health and Safety Rating System apart from overcrowding, is planned for 2027.

The deadlines social landlords must meet

The timescales themselves aren't changing. They simply apply to more hazards:

  • Emergency hazards: investigate and complete the safety work within 24 hours of becoming aware
  • Significant hazards: investigate within 10 working days
  • Written summary to the tenant: within 3 working days of the investigation ending
  • Safety work: within 5 working days of the investigation ending
  • Longer-term preventative work: start within 5 working days or, if that isn't possible, as soon as reasonably practicable and within 12 weeks

For the working-day deadlines, day one is the day after the landlord becomes aware. That is why a dated report in writing is so useful.

Why excess cold matters if you have damp

In a real house, cold and damp are rarely two separate problems. Warm, moist air turns to water on cold surfaces, and that is where mould grows. I've covered how to spot it in black mould: condensation or rising damp?

The guidance itself makes the connection. It gives a lack of thermal insulation leading to excessive mould growth as an example of a defect, and a broken window or door as disrepair that could lead to excess cold. It also says that where a damp and mould hazard comes from missing ventilation or poor insulation, the landlord must take appropriate remedial action.

So a mould wash and a coat of stain-block is not the whole answer if the same wall is going to go mouldy again next winter. Plenty of North East homes are worth a close look on this score, particularly solid-walled terraces and older Tyneside flats, where cold walls and limited ventilation can catch people out.

If you're a council or housing association tenant

  • Report it in writing by email or through the landlord's portal, and keep a copy
  • Take dated photos and note which rooms are affected and how it is affecting your household
  • Mention anyone vulnerable, such as young children or someone with a health condition
  • Ask for the written summary if the deadline passes
  • Allow access for inspections and repairs, and keep a note of any visits

If the landlord doesn't comply, start with a formal complaint. You can then escalate to the Housing Ombudsman. Awaab's Law is also an implied term of your tenancy, so you may be able to go to court for breach of contract. Get advice from Shelter or a housing solicitor before taking that step.

Who isn't covered

The law applies to almost all social housing let under a tenancy by a registered provider in England. It doesn't cover housing occupied under a licence, long leaseholders or shared owners. Scotland and Wales are outside these regulations.

If you're a private landlord or tenant

Awaab's Law doesn't apply to private rentals yet. The Renters' Rights Act 2025 contains the powers, and the government is consulting on how to use them until 18 December 2026. The start date will be informed by that consultation, so treat any firm date you see quoted with caution.

That doesn't mean private tenants have no protection. Landlords already have duties under the Homes (Fitness for Human Habitation) Act 2018 and the Housing Act 2004. If you're a landlord, dealing with damp properly now makes sense whatever the final rules look like.

If you manage social housing

The tight deadlines leave little room for repeat visits and guesswork. A vague finding of "damp" doesn't tell anyone what to fix. What helps is a clear record of what was found, where, the likely cause and what's needed to stop it coming back, whether that's a repair outside, better ventilation or more insulation.

When to get a survey

A survey makes sense when the cause isn't clear, when mould keeps returning after repairs, or when a landlord or agent needs an independent diagnosis and written report. It is also worth having before paying for damp-proofing work that may not address the cause.

If you're a social housing tenant, ask your landlord to investigate first. That's its job, and you shouldn't normally need to pay for this yourself.

Common questions

Does Awaab's Law cover damp and mould already?

Yes. Damp and mould hazards in social housing in England have been covered since 27 October 2025. Phase two adds other hazards, including excess cold. It does not replace the damp and mould rules.

Does Awaab's Law apply to private landlords?

Not yet. The powers are in the Renters' Rights Act 2025, but the government is still consulting on how to apply them, with responses open until 18 December 2026. Private landlords already have legal duties on housing standards under other laws.

Should a council or housing association tenant pay for their own damp survey?

Normally not as a first step. Under Awaab's Law it is the landlord's job to investigate a significant hazard within 10 working days. Report it in writing first, and take advice from Shelter or a housing solicitor if you are considering your own report for a dispute.
