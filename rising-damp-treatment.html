Short answer: true rising damp is groundwater drawn up through the bottom of a wall because the damp-proof course is missing, has broken down or is bridged. It is less common than many people are told: a lot of damp low on North East walls is actually a bridged damp-proof course, raised ground or path levels, a leak, penetrating damp or condensation. Northeast Damp Proofers confirms the cause first with moisture readings and an inspection inside and out, and only recommends a new injected damp-proof course and replastering when rising damp is the real problem. Signs of rising damp

Rising damp affects the base of ground-floor walls. The typical signs are:

  • A tide mark or damp band low on the wall, usually up to about a metre high
  • Crumbling or blown plaster and a white, fluffy deposit of salts on the surface
  • Peeling paint or wallpaper lifting at skirting level
  • Skirting boards that are stained, soft or rotting
  • Damp that is there all year round rather than coming and going with the weather

Black mould on ceilings, in corners and around windows is usually condensation, not rising damp. A patch that gets worse after heavy rain points to penetrating damp. Our guide Condensation or rising damp? explains the differences.

Why it is often not rising damp

A damp meter reading on its own does not prove rising damp. Common pin meters are designed for timber and read high on salts, foil-backed paper and some plasters, so a "red" reading can mislead; see Damp meter says rising damp? Why the reading can mislead you. Before recommending treatment we also look for:

  • Bridging: a path, patio, flower bed or render that sits above the damp-proof course lets water bypass it. Lowering the ground or cutting back the render can be the whole fix. More in Has my damp-proof course failed? Why it's often just bridged.
  • Leaks: a dripping pipe, a failed shower seal or a leaking downpipe can soak the base of a wall.
  • Penetrating damp: defective pointing, gutters or render on an exposed gable.
  • Blocked air bricks: under a suspended timber floor these let moisture build up, and can lead to rot; see Should you block air bricks in winter?
  • Old salts: salts left in a wall from past damp can keep drawing moisture from the air and spoil new plaster, even after the original cause has gone; see Damp came back after replastering?

Rising damp in North East homes

  • Victorian and Edwardian terraces across Newcastle, Gateshead and Sunderland often have original slate or bitumen damp-proof courses, or none at all, and many have had yards and paths raised over the years.
  • Tyneside flats have shared ground-floor walls, so the downstairs flat often sees damp first; see Damp in a Tyneside flat? Upstairs, downstairs and who fixes what.
  • Former colliery houses in County Durham and Northumberland are frequently solid brick with patched-up damp-proof courses from earlier treatments.
  • Stone cottages and older stone houses were built to breathe. Cement render, cement pointing and plastic paints trap moisture in the wall, and the answer is often lime and breathable materials rather than chemicals; see Damp in an old stone cottage?

How we diagnose and treat it

  1. Inspection inside and out. We check ground levels, the existing damp-proof course, render, pointing, gutters, downpipes, air bricks and any plumbing near the damp.
  2. Moisture readings and a pattern check. We map where the damp is and how high it goes, using calibrated meters and looking at the pattern rather than a single number.
  3. A plain-English diagnosis on site. We tell you what is causing it. If it isn't rising damp, we say so and recommend the right fix instead.
  4. Treatment if it is rising damp. We remove salt-contaminated plaster, inject a new chemical damp-proof course into the mortar course, and replaster with a salt-resistant system so the finish lasts.
  5. Making good. Skirting boards and any damaged timber are replaced as needed, and we'll advise on drying-out times before you decorate.

Common questions

How do I know if I have rising damp?

Look for a tide mark low on a ground-floor wall, salts and crumbling plaster, and damp that is there all year. The only reliable way to know is an inspection that checks the outside of the wall and rules out bridging, leaks and condensation, rather than a single damp meter reading.

Do I need a new damp-proof course?

Only if the existing one has failed or there isn't one, and the damp is genuinely rising from the ground. Many damp-proof courses haven't failed at all but are bridged by raised paths, soil or render. Fix the bridge and the wall can dry out without injection.

Why has the damp come back after a new damp-proof course?

Usually because the original cause wasn't rising damp, the wall was replastered with ordinary gypsum plaster over salt-contaminated brickwork, or the new damp-proof course has since been bridged. We look at what was done and why it hasn't worked.

How long does a wall take to dry after treatment?

It varies with the wall thickness and material, but solid walls dry slowly, often over several months. We'll give you guidance on when it is safe to decorate and what finishes to use in the meantime.
