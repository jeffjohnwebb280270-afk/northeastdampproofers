Why North Shields and Tynemouth homes get damp

The river, the sea and the lie of the land shape most damp problems here:

  • Houses built into the banks. North Shields climbs steeply from the Fish Quay to the old upper town, and many homes on the banks have ground, yards or retaining walls higher than the floor inside. Water pushes in sideways through the masonry. That's not rising damp, and an injected damp-proof course won't hold it back; drainage, lowering ground or a waterproofing system usually will. Our guide to damp cellars, tanking and membranes explains the options.
  • Georgian and Victorian townhouses around Northumberland Square and Howard Street. Solid walls, tall chimneys, basements and suspended timber floors. Much of the old centre is in conservation areas, so check with North Tyneside Council before changing anything outside. See damp patches on chimney breasts and why blocking air bricks can rot your floor.
  • Tyneside flats across North Shields and Wallsend. Ground-floor flats often get damp low on the walls from a bridged damp-proof course or a yard concreted up too high, while ceiling stains usually start in the flat above. Read damp in a Tyneside flat: upstairs, downstairs and who fixes what.
  • Sea-facing terraces in Tynemouth and Cullercoats. Homes near Front Street, the Longsands and Cullercoats Bay take wind-driven rain and salty air off the North Sea. Worn pointing, cracked render, bay roofs and chimney flashings let water in, and salt keeps walls damp for longer. Our guide to damp patches after heavy rain covers the usual culprits.
  • Edwardian and inter-war homes in Whitley Bay, Monkseaton and Preston. Bay-fronted terraces and semis, often pebbledashed. Cracked render traps water behind it, and damp under bay windows is common. Where walls have been coated in cement or plastic paint, see why cement and plastic paint trap damp in older walls.
  • Post-war and modern estates. Much of Chirton, Battle Hill, Killingworth and Longbenton has cavity walls, where true rising damp is rare. Black mould is usually condensation, sometimes worsened by cold lintels, problem cavity insulation or extractor fans that don't work. See damp after cavity wall insulation and black mould: condensation or rising damp?.

Common questions

The back wall of my North Shields house is damp but the front is fine. Is it rising damp?

Often not. Many houses on the banks have higher ground or a yard against the back wall, so water soaks in sideways, and blocked gullies make it worse. An injected damp-proof course won't stop that. Drainage, lowering the ground or a waterproofing system usually will, and a survey with moisture readings shows which.

My Tynemouth or Whitley Bay house gets damp after storms. What's causing it?

Damp that appears after wind and heavy rain, especially on sea-facing walls, bays and chimney breasts, is usually penetrating damp. Worn pointing, cracked render, bay roofs, window seals and chimney flashings are the common causes. Fixing the outside of the wall and letting it dry out is normally the answer.

I rent in North Tyneside and have damp and mould. What should I do?

Report it to your landlord in writing first, as they're responsible for dealing with damp and mould hazards. Council and housing association landlords now have strict timescales under Awaab's Law, and private tenants have rights too: see private tenant with damp and mould. Landlords, letting agents and housing providers can book us for an independent survey and report.
