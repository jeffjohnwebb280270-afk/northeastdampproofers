Damp-proof course guarantee: what it covers and is it worth anything?

By Jeff Webb, PCA Qualified Technician in Damp & Timber · 23 November 2026

Short answer: a damp-proofing guarantee is only as good as the company behind it and the wording on the paper. A standard guarantee is a promise from the contractor to come back and put right faults in their own work, usually for faulty materials or workmanship. If that firm has closed, the promise normally goes with it, unless the guarantee was backed by insurance. Even a valid guarantee rarely covers damp from a new cause, like a blocked gutter, raised ground outside or condensation. So treat a guarantee as useful paperwork, not as proof that a house is dry.

This comes up a lot when people are buying in the North East. A solicitor asks the seller for "the damp-proof guarantee", the seller finds a certificate from 2009, and nobody is sure whether it's worth anything. Here's how to read one honestly.

What a damp-proofing guarantee usually covers

Most guarantees for an injected damp-proof course, tanking or a membrane system are worded around the same idea: if the treatment the company carried out fails because of faulty materials or faulty workmanship, they'll come back and fix it. Typically that means:

  • The treated walls only, as shown on the original plan or survey drawing
  • Failure of that specific treatment, not damp in general
  • A set period, commonly 10 or 20 years from the date of the work
  • Conditions you have to meet, such as keeping the original paperwork and not altering the treated walls

What it usually doesn't cover

This is where most disappointment comes from. A guarantee for a damp-proof course doesn't stop a house from getting damp for other reasons. Expect it to exclude things like:

  • Bridging: soil, paths or patios built up against the wall above the damp-proof course, or render taken down to the ground
  • Penetrating damp: leaking gutters and downpipes, failed pointing, cracked render, or a leaking chimney (see damp patches after heavy rain)
  • Condensation and mould from cooking, drying washing and poor ventilation
  • Plumbing leaks and flooding
  • Later work by someone else, such as new plaster, drilling, or a kitchen fitted against the treated wall
  • Walls that weren't treated in the first place

So if damp shows up years later, the first question isn't "is it under guarantee?" but "what's actually causing it?" A lot of damp in treated walls turns out to be something the original work was never meant to fix.

Contractor guarantee or insurance-backed guarantee?

There are two kinds, and it makes a big difference which one you've got.

  • Contractor's guarantee: a promise from the company itself. It only works while that company is still trading and willing to honour it. If it has closed down, there's usually nobody to claim against.
  • Insurance-backed guarantee (IBG): an insurance policy that sits behind the contractor's guarantee. The Property Care Association (PCA) explains that an IBG will honour the terms of the original guarantee if faults arise and the contractor has since ceased to trade. You should have a separate certificate or policy document for it.

The PCA has also said that defects caused by poor work usually show up within the first two or three years. By the time a 20-year guarantee is 15 years old, a new damp problem is more often down to something that has changed since, not the original treatment.

Buying a house with a damp guarantee

If the seller has a guarantee, it's worth having, but check it rather than relying on it. Ask your solicitor or the seller for:

  • The original guarantee and the plan showing exactly which walls were treated
  • The company's details, and whether it is still trading (you can check on Companies House)
  • Any insurance-backed certificate, if one was issued, and who the insurer is
  • Whether it transfers to a new owner and what you need to do. Some guarantees need the company to be told of a change of ownership, and some firms charge to transfer them. Read the terms.
  • The original survey or invoice, so you can see why the work was done

Then look at the house itself. Are the walls dry? Has the ground outside been raised? Are the gutters sound? A guarantee on a wall that's damp for a different reason is no help. If your homebuyer survey has flagged damp, our guide to damp on a homebuyer survey covers what to do next.

Thinking of making a claim?

If you believe a treatment has failed and the company is still around:

  1. Find the guarantee, the plan and the invoice.
  2. Rule out the obvious other causes first: gutters, downpipes, ground levels, leaks and condensation.
  3. Write to the company with dates and photos, and ask them to inspect.
  4. If the company has closed and you have an insurance-backed certificate, contact the insurer named on it.

Expect the company to inspect and to check for the exclusions above. That's normal, and it's why an independent view of the cause helps before you start.

Getting new work done? What to ask for

If you're paying for damp treatment now, the guarantee should be part of your decision, not an afterthought. Before you sign:

  • Ask to see a copy of the guarantee wording before the work, not after
  • Ask exactly what's covered and what's excluded
  • Ask whether it's insurance-backed, and who the insurer is
  • Get a written specification and a plan of the walls being treated
  • Make sure the treatment matches the cause. A long guarantee on a damp-proof course does nothing for a leaking gutter or condensation, and a firm that offers one for a problem that isn't rising damp is a warning sign (see has my damp-proof course failed?)

When to get a survey

An independent damp survey is worth it when:

  • Damp has come back in walls that were treated before, and you want to know the real cause before you claim
  • You're buying a house with an old damp guarantee and want to know whether the walls are actually dry
  • You've been quoted for a new damp-proof course and want a second opinion first

Common questions

Is a damp-proof guarantee transferable when I buy a house?

Often, but it depends on the wording. Some guarantees transfer automatically, others need the company to be told of the change of ownership, and some firms charge a fee. Ask for the original document and read the terms, and get your solicitor to check them.

What happens to my damp guarantee if the company goes bust?

A plain contractor's guarantee usually ends with the company, as there's nobody left to honour it. If the work came with an insurance-backed guarantee, the insurer should honour the original guarantee terms. Look for a separate insurance certificate with the paperwork.

Does a damp guarantee mean the house won't get damp?

No. It only covers failure of the specific treatment, on the walls that were treated, because of faulty materials or workmanship. Damp from leaking gutters, raised ground, plumbing leaks or condensation is normally excluded.
