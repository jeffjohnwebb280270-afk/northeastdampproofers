Why Gateshead homes get damp

Gateshead packs a lot of different housing into one borough, and the land rises steeply from the river. The damp we're asked about usually comes down to one of these:

  • Tyneside flats in Bensham, Saltwell, Shipcote and Deckham. Pairs of upstairs and downstairs flats behind side-by-side front doors, with back yards and shared walls. Damp at the bottom of downstairs walls is often a bridged damp-proof course or a yard that has been concreted up too high, while stains on ceilings and party walls can come from the flat above.
  • Victorian and Edwardian terraces and villas in Low Fell, Saltwell and Sheriff Hill. Solid brick walls, suspended timber floors, bay windows and tall chimney stacks. Cement render, hard pointing and plastic masonry paint can trap moisture in old walls. Our guide on why cement and plastic paint trap damp in older solid walls explains what to look for.
  • Hillside homes in Whickham, Winlaton, Ryton, Crawcrook and Rowlands Gill. Older stone cottages and houses built into the Derwent and Tyne valley slopes. Where ground at the back sits above the floor inside, water pushes sideways through the wall. That's penetrating damp, and a chemical damp-proof course won't stop it.
  • Former pit and industrial villages. Felling, Heworth, Pelaw, Dunston, Teams and Birtley have rows of older terraces with solid walls that are cold in winter and prone to condensation and black mould on outside walls and in corners.
  • Council estates, flats and tower blocks. Post-war estates such as Leam Lane, Wrekenton and Beacon Lough, plus Gateshead's high-rise blocks, mostly have cavity or concrete walls. True rising damp is rare. Black mould is usually condensation, sometimes made worse by cold bridges at lintels and balconies, failed cavity insulation or extractor fans that don't work. See damp after cavity wall insulation if your walls were filled.

Older Gateshead houses often have coal cellars too. Our guide on damp cellars, tanking and membranes explains what's normal and when waterproofing is worth it.

Common questions

Is the damp in my ground-floor Tyneside flat in Bensham coming from the flat upstairs?

Sometimes, but not always. In a Tyneside flat the upper flat's bathroom and kitchen often sit above your rooms, so a leaking waste pipe, shower tray or bath seal can show up as a stain on your ceiling or down a party wall. Damp low down on walls is more often a bridged damp-proof course, high ground in the back yard or a leaking downpipe. Moisture readings and the pattern of the damp tell us which, so the right flat owner fixes the right thing.

My house in Whickham, Ryton or Winlaton is built into a hillside. Why is the back wall damp?

Where a house is cut into a slope, the ground at the back can sit well above the inside floor level. A damp-proof course can't stop water pushing in from soil banked against a wall, so this is lateral penetrating damp rather than rising damp. Depending on the cause, the fix might be drainage and lowering the ground outside, or a waterproofing system on the inside. We'll tell you which, and whether chemical injection would help at all (it usually wouldn't). If damp appears after storms instead, see damp patch after heavy rain.

I rent my home in Gateshead and have black mould. What should I do?

Report it to your landlord in writing first, as they're responsible for dealing with damp and mould hazards. Council and housing association landlords now have strict timescales under Awaab's Law, and private tenants have rights too: see private tenant with damp and mould. Black mould is usually condensation, but cold walls, leaks or blocked extractor fans make it worse. Landlords, letting agents and housing providers can book us for an independent survey and report. Our guide Black mould in winter: condensation or rising damp? explains the difference.
